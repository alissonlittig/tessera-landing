"We had 99.95% uptime last quarter" is a fine sentence for a slide. It is a poor one for a customer who lost their evening to the other 0.05%.

## What the nines really allow

Allowed downtime is easy to compute and surprisingly small over a month.

| Target | Per 30 days | Per year |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 99% | 7 h 12 min | 3 d 15 h |
| 99.9% | 43 min | 8 h 45 min |
| 99.99% | 4 min 19 s | 52 min |

A single bad deploy can spend a whole quarter's budget at 99.99%. That is not a reason to avoid the target. It is a reason to know what you are promising.

## Measure from the outside

Health checks that run inside your own network will tell you the process is alive. They will not tell you that DNS is broken or that a certificate expired. Probe from several regions, over the public internet, against the same URL a customer uses.

<Callout>
Require two regions to agree before opening an incident. It cuts false alarms without adding more than a few seconds of delay.
</Callout>

## Report per component

An overall percentage hides the part customers care about. Split the number by component, such as API, dashboard and billing, and let people follow only what they depend on. A partial outage in webhooks should not make the whole product look red, and it should not be invisible either.

## Show the bad days

Honest history builds more trust than a spotless chart. Keep 90 days of incidents public, with start, end and a one-line summary. Customers read gaps as concealment faster than they read downtime as failure.
